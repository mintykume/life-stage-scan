# The six layers

Use this file during Step 3 (The layered read). Each layer lists what to read, the signals to look for, framework notes with their limits, and common misreads. Full citations are in `sources.md`.

## 1. Season & situation (the external stage)

**What to read:**

- **Life roles held now and the weight of each.** Super's life-span, life-space theory names nine major roles: child, student, leisurite, citizen, worker, spouse or partner, homemaker, parent and pensioner. Roles compete for time and energy, and a role can grow or shrink without the others noticing.
- **Career season.** Super's stages are growth, exploration, establishment, maintenance and disengagement. Adults recycle through them at transitions. This skill uses four working labels: *exploring*, *establishing*, *maintaining* and *reinventing* (a recycle).
- **Transition phase, if one is underway** (Bridges):
  - *Ending:* letting go of an old identity or situation.
  - *Neutral zone:* in-between, disoriented, unusually creative.
  - *New beginning:* commitment to the new form.
- **Resources and constraints:** time, money, location, obligations, energy, support.

**Signals:**

- Which roles they listed first.
- Which roles they never mentioned.
- Words like "stuck," "between" or "after X ends."

**Common misreads:**

- Treating the neutral zone as a problem to end quickly. Its real task is usually to experiment; pushing a premature decision wastes the most creative phase.
- Treating exploration at any age as failure to commit.
- Keeping someone exploring forever when the evidence says they've found it and need to commit.
- Ignoring constraints. A plan that assumes time or money the person doesn't have is a fantasy.

## 2. Build (temperament and energy)

This is McAdams level 1, dispositional traits.

**What to read:**

- What energizes and what drains them.
- Their natural pace: sprints or steady.
- Appetite for people.
- Novelty versus depth.
- Structure versus freedom.
- How they recover.

**Signals:** read these from what they *do*: the time-loss moments, how they spend a free day, which tasks they procrastinate on.

**Notes:**

- Personality-test labels (types, letters, colors) are fine if the person brings them as a self-description. Don't hand them new labels.
- Describe the person in plain behavioral terms: "you recharge alone after people-heavy days" rather than "you're an introvert."

**Common misreads:**

- Recommending a plan that fights the build, such as a hustle plan for a reflective person or a solitary plan for someone who needs a team.

## 3. Strivings (goals, values, skills, coping)

This is McAdams level 2, characteristic adaptations.

**What to read:**

- **Current goals and the values behind them.** Check whether the goals are theirs or borrowed.
- **Readiness per goal**, using the stages of change (transtheoretical model):
  - *precontemplation:* not considering change
  - *contemplation:* thinking about it
  - *preparation:* getting ready
  - *action:* doing it
  - *maintenance:* keeping it going

  A plan that assumes action when someone is still in contemplation fails. For contemplation, the right move is a small experiment that produces information, not a commitment.
- **Capital:** rare and valuable skills, proof (things they've made that others can see), relationships and reputation already built. Note what's *compounding*: what gets more valuable the longer they keep doing it (Newport's "career capital").
- **Coping habits:** what they do under stress, and whether it helps.

**Signals:**

- What people ask them for help with: capital they may not see.
- What they've kept doing for years without being made to.

**Common misreads:**

- Confusing busy with progress.
- Counting credentials as capital when proof would serve them better.

## 4. Meaning-making (whose standards run the show)

This layer uses Kegan's orders of mind.

**What to read:**

- **Socialized mind.** "Good enough" comes mostly from people and institutions they're loyal to. Strength: belonging, loyalty, responsiveness. Limit: conflicting expectations feel like being torn apart.
- **Self-authoring mind.** An internal compass that can hold a position against approval. Strength: direction, independence. Limit: it can get locked inside its own system.
- **Self-transforming mind.** Can hold several value systems at once and see the limits of their own. This is rarer.

**Signals:**

- How they decided last time they got conflicting advice.
- Whose disappointment would hurt most.
- Whether "should" sentences have a named source ("my parents think…") or an internal one ("I've decided…").

**Notes and limits:**

- Formal assessment requires a trained interviewer using the Subject-Object Interview. Here it is always a hypothesis, flagged as **[read]** with low or medium confidence.
- Many people are between two orders. Say so rather than forcing a category.
- The most useful output isn't a stage label. It's naming one thing the person is currently *inside of* and can't see (for example "doing well = being praised by teachers"), so it becomes something they can look at and choose.
- Never present this as a ranking of the person's worth or maturity.

**Common misreads:**

- Reading family duty, faith or community commitment as automatically "socialized." People can choose those values from a self-authored place. Ask how they relate to the obligation.

## 5. Story (narrative identity and life theme)

This is McAdams level 3, plus Savickas's idea of the life theme.

**What to read:**

- **Recurring themes:** agency (mastery, achievement, influence) and communion (love, care, belonging).
- **Sequences:** *redemption*, where bad turns into good, versus *contamination*, where good gets spoiled. People who tell more redemption sequences tend to report higher wellbeing (McAdams et al., 2001), but don't force redemption onto a story that hasn't earned it.
- **Whose script they're living:** their own, a parent's, a culture's, an algorithm's.
- **What the role models add up to.** Take the qualities they admired across all three and look for the common thread. That's often the self they're building.
- **The favorite story's plot.** Which part did they emphasize? The escape, the mastery, the found family, the return?
- **The life theme.** Savickas describes careers as a way of actively mastering what one once passively suffered, turning a preoccupation into an occupation.

**Signals:**

- The chapter title they gave.
- The saying they tell themselves.
- What they complain about repeatedly.

**Common misreads:**

- Taking the story at face value. The story is evidence about how they make sense of things, not a factual record. Compare it to the behavior.

## 6. Tensions (where the layers meet)

Compare the layers. Look for:

- **Stated versus revealed preferences:** what they say they value against where their time, money and attention go.
- **Story versus evidence:** "I'm not creative" next to three things they made this month.
- **Strivings versus build:** goals that need a temperament they don't have, or ignore one they do.
- **Season versus ambition:** establishment moves made mid-exploration, or endless exploration when the evidence says commit.
- **Inherited script versus emerging self:** the path that makes others proud against the moments they come alive.
- **Readiness versus plan:** a big commitment planned at the contemplation stage.

Pick the **one or two tensions that explain the most**. A reading with seven tensions has none.

## Synthesis

**Chapter name.** Evocative but accurate, and specific to this person. Good: "The Apprentice Who Outgrew the Workshop," "Reliable, Rehearsing in Secret." Weak: "A Time of Growth."

**What this chapter is for.** Name the developmental task of the season in one paragraph. Examples:

- Neutral zone: run experiments, not commitments.
- Establishment: build proof.
- Recycle: carry the best of the old self into the new form.

**The core tension.** One sentence, then the evidence for it.

**The leverage point.** Look for a move that:

- resolves or uses the core tension
- fits the build
- matches the readiness stage
- builds compounding capital
- is reversible enough to try now

Often it's a small, public, regular version of the thing they come alive doing, made in a way that uses a strength they already trust.

**Evidence labels.** Tag each claim inline:

- **[said]** they told you
- **[shown]** behavior they described
- **[read]** your inference

Add a confidence level (low, medium or high). Meaning-making claims are rarely above medium.

**Flattery check.** Before you finish, ask: does every layer read as good news? Is any uncomfortable pattern missing because it would be awkward to say? If so, add it, kindly and with evidence.
