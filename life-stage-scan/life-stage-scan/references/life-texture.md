# Life texture modules

Use this file during Step 6 (Life texture). Pick the two or three modules that matter most for this person; a reading with every module is a catalogue, not a reading.

## Romanticize

**Grounding.**

- **Savoring.** Bryant & Veroff call savoring the "positive counterpart of coping." It means deliberately attending to, appreciating and lengthening good experiences, in three directions:
  - *Anticipating:* looking forward. Planning a good thing is part of the good thing.
  - *Being in it:* sharpening attention while it happens (senses, sharing, slowing down).
  - *Reminiscing:* revisiting it afterwards (a log, photos, retelling).
- **Ikigai.** In its original Japanese sense, ikigai is small and daily: whatever makes life feel worth living. The popular four-circle Venn diagram (love, good at, world needs, paid for) is a Western "purpose" diagram that later picked up the word. Look for the daily version: the small things that make an ordinary Tuesday worth it.

**Build from their life, not generic self-care.** Draw on their story, themes, city, climate, season and constraints. Typically include:

- **A daily micro-ritual** of 10 minutes or less, attached to something that already happens (coffee, commute, the walk home).
- **A small planned adventure each week**, named so it can be anticipated.
- **A seasonal tradition** fitted to their place: what's specific about this month where they live?
- **A capture-and-reminisce habit:** a one-line log, a photo series with a rule, a jar of ticket stubs.
- **A sensory signature:** a light, a scent, a playlist, a particular cup. These cue the state.

**Rules.**

- **Free versions.** Always include them. Romanticizing is attention, not spending.
- **Real life, not escape.** Romanticize the life they actually have. If a ritual is doing the job of avoiding something, say so gently.
- **Specific, from their own story.** Tie each idea to something they said or showed, and label it.

> **Generic (cut it):** "Start a gratitude journal and take more walks."
>
> **Specific:** "Your best weeks are the ones where you build something late at night [shown]. Your favorite story is a heist film about a crew of specialists [said]. Make Thursday 'lab night': same playlist, desk lamp only, one small build, three lines in a log about what worked. You'll look forward to it, sink into it, and have a record to look back on."

## Personal style

**Frame.** Style is the costume for the next chapter. Derive it from the story layer (who they're becoming), then fit it to:

- their real days: climate, commute, activities, dress codes
- their budget
- what they already own

**Offer:**

- **A three-word style statement**, such as "archivist with a pulse" or "quiet, sharp, warm." Every purchase gets checked against it.
- **A palette, reasoned from principles:**
  - *Contrast:* the difference between their hair, skin and eyes; high-contrast people often carry high-contrast outfits.
  - *Value:* light to dark.
  - *Undertone:* warm, cool or neutral.
  - *Chroma:* soft to bright.

  Seasonal color analysis is a fun starting lens and many people enjoy it, but there is little peer-reviewed research behind it. Present it as a hypothesis to test: hold fabrics near the face in daylight and compare.
- **Silhouette principles** that work with the body as it is: line, proportion, texture, fit, how fabric moves.
- **One high-leverage piece to add**, the item that would change the most outfits, and **one to retire**.
- **A signature detail**, if they want one: hair, a type of accessory, a scent, a color they always wear.
- **Budget tiers:** what to do with nothing (restyle and edit what they own, tailoring), a little (secondhand, one piece), and more (investment pieces).

**Rules.**

- **Never suggest changing the body** (weight, shape, features) as styling advice, even if the person brings it up. Dress the body they have now. If they mention wanting to change their body first, acknowledge it without endorsing the premise, and show how good the style can look now.
- **Photos.** For specific color advice, a daylight photo helps. Offer, don't require.
- **Trends.** Give trend-aware picks only if they ask, filtered through their identity. If you name current trends, search first.

## Explore & learn

- **One compounding skill**, tied to the main move. It should get more valuable the longer they practice it.
- **One wonder topic**, purely for curiosity, with no purpose required. If `book-mode` is available, offer it for this.
- **Three culture picks** (books, films, music, places) that match their themes or chapter. Check each one exists and is what you think it is; never invent a title or misattribute an author.
- **One experiment in a new environment:** a club, a class, a neighborhood, a meetup, a city. New rooms produce new information.

## On-request modules

- **Environment:** room, desk, digital space. One change that makes the main move easier, one that makes rest easier.
- **People & community:** where their kind of people gather, online and offline. Include one way to meet them by contributing rather than networking.
- **Energy:** sleep, movement and food at a general level only. No diets, calorie or weight numbers, or supplement advice; point to professionals for health questions.
- **Money:** general principles and options only. Note that you're not a financial advisor. Search before stating any current figure, rate or rule.
